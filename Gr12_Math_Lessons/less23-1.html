Introduction to Logarithmic Functions

Understanding logarithms and their relationship to exponential functions as inverse functions.

📚 Core Concepts & Theory

The Birth of Logarithms

Finding the inverse of an exponential function led to an equation where making \( y \) the subject was problematic using traditional methods. For example:

  • If \( y = 2^x \) is our function \( f \), then \( x = 2^y \) is its inverse \( f^{-1} \)
  • How do we solve for \( y \) when \( y \) is in the exponent?

In the late 1500s–early 1600s, mathematician John Napier devised a way to make \( y \) the subject. He introduced a new notation called a logarithm, creating the logarithmic function: \( \log_a x \).

Understanding Logarithms

The logarithm (log) of a number is based on the definition of exponents.

Key Insight: The log of a number, with regard to a given base, is the exponent of that base which will give you that number.

Using numbers makes it clearer:

\[ 8 = 2^{\color{blue}3} \]

The exponent 3 is the logarithm of 8 to base 2

We write this in logarithmic form as:

\[ \log_2 8 = 3 \]

📐 Definition of Logarithm

For \( a > 0 \) and \( a \neq 1 \), as well as \( x > 0 \):

\[ y = \log_a x \iff x = a^y \]

In words: The logarithm of \( x \) to base \( a \) equals \( y \) means that \( a \) raised to the power \( y \) equals \( x \).

Restrictions on Logarithms:
  • The base \( a \) cannot be negative, zero, or 1 (i.e., \( a > 0, a \neq 1 \))
  • The argument \( x \) (the number inside the log) must be greater than zero (i.e., \( x > 0 \))
  • \( \log_a 0 \) has no meaning because no power of \( a \) can equal zero

Special Logarithm Values

Property Logarithmic Form Reason (Exponential Form)
Log of the base \( \log_a a = 1 \) Because \( a^1 = a \)
Log of one \( \log_a 1 = 0 \) Because \( a^0 = 1 \)
Power rule \( \log_a x^m = m \log_a x \) E.g., \( \log 7^2 = 2 \log 7 \)

Logarithmic Functions as Inverses

Now we can make \( y \) the subject of the formula in equations like \( x = 2^y \):

\[ x = 2^y \implies \log_2 x = y \]

Therefore, the inverse of an exponential function is a logarithmic function.

  • If \( f(x) = 2^x \), then \( f^{-1}(x) = \log_2 x \)
  • If \( f(x) = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^x \), then \( f^{-1}(x) = \log_{1/2} x \)

The exponential function and its inverse (log function) are symmetrical about the line \( y = x \).

Characteristics of \( y = \log_a x \)

When \( a > 1 \) (Increasing)

  • Graph increases from left to right
  • As \( x \) increases, \( y \) increases
  • Examples: \( \log_2 x, \log_{10} x \)

When \( 0 < a < 1 \) (Decreasing)

  • Graph decreases from left to right
  • As \( x \) increases, \( y \) decreases
  • Examples: \( \log_{1/2} x, \log_{1/3} x \)

All logarithmic functions share these properties:

  • Domain: \( x \in (0; \infty) \) — only positive \( x \) values
  • Range: \( y \in (-\infty; \infty) \) — all real numbers
  • No \( y \)-intercept (graph never crosses the \( y \)-axis)
  • \( x \)-intercept: Always at \( (1; 0) \) since \( \log_a 1 = 0 \)
  • Vertical asymptote: The \( y \)-axis (\( x = 0 \))
  • One-to-one function: Each input gives a unique output
The expression \( \log_a x \) is defined only if \( a > 0 \), \( a \neq 1 \), AND \( x > 0 \). Always check these conditions before evaluating a logarithm!

💡 Worked Examples

▼

Example 1: Converting Exponential Form to Logarithmic Form

Convert each exponential equation to logarithmic form:

  1. Convert \( 7^2 = 49 \)
    Base = 7, Exponent = 2, Result = 49
    Answer: \( \log_7 49 = 2 \)
  2. Convert \( 10^3 = 1000 \)
    Base = 10, Exponent = 3, Result = 1000
    Answer: \( \log_{10} 1000 = 3 \) (or simply \( \log 1000 = 3 \))
  3. Convert \( 2^{-2} = \frac{1}{4} \)
    Base = 2, Exponent = -2, Result = \( \frac{1}{4} \)
    Answer: \( \log_2 \frac{1}{4} = -2 \)
  4. Convert \( 25^{\frac{1}{2}} = 5 \)
    Base = 25, Exponent = \( \frac{1}{2} \), Result = 5
    Answer: \( \log_{25} 5 = \frac{1}{2} \)

Example 2: Converting Logarithmic Form to Exponential Form

Convert each logarithmic equation to exponential form:

  1. Convert \( \log_2 8 = 3 \)
    Base = 2, Logarithm (exponent) = 3, Argument = 8
    Answer: \( 2^3 = 8 \)
  2. Convert \( \log_5 125 = 3 \)
    Base = 5, Logarithm = 3, Argument = 125
    Answer: \( 5^3 = 125 \)
  3. Convert \( \log_8 2 = \frac{1}{3} \)
    Base = 8, Logarithm = \( \frac{1}{3} \), Argument = 2
    Answer: \( 8^{\frac{1}{3}} = 2 \)
  4. Convert \( \log_2 0.125 = -3 \)
    Base = 2, Logarithm = -3, Argument = 0.125
    Answer: \( 2^{-3} = 0.125 \) (since \( 0.125 = \frac{1}{8} \))

Example 3: Finding Inverses of Exponential Functions

Find the inverse of each function and express in logarithmic form:

  1. Find the inverse of \( f(x) = 2^x \)
    Step 1: Replace \( f(x) \) with \( y \): \( y = 2^x \)
    Step 2: Swap \( x \) and \( y \): \( x = 2^y \)
    Step 3: Make \( y \) the subject using logs: \( y = \log_2 x \)
    Answer: \( f^{-1}(x) = \log_2 x \)
  2. Find the inverse of \( g(x) = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^x \)
    Step 1: Replace \( g(x) \) with \( y \): \( y = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^x \)
    Step 2: Swap \( x \) and \( y \): \( x = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^y \)
    Step 3: Make \( y \) the subject: \( y = \log_{\frac{1}{2}} x \)
    Answer: \( g^{-1}(x) = \log_{\frac{1}{2}} x \)
  3. Identify the characteristics of \( y = \log_2 x \)
    • Domain: \( x > 0 \) or \( x \in (0; \infty) \)
    • Range: \( y \in \mathbb{R} \) or \( y \in (-\infty; \infty) \)
    • \( x \)-intercept: \( (1; 0) \)
    • No \( y \)-intercept
    • Asymptote: \( x = 0 \) (the \( y \)-axis)
    • Graph is increasing (base > 1)
Pattern to Remember: The exponent in exponential form becomes the answer in log form, and they always share the same base! Think of it as: "Base raised to the Log equals the Argument" — \( a^{\log_a x} = x \)

Question 1: Express \( 7^2 = 49 \) in logarithmic form.

A \( \log_2 49 = 7 \)
B \( \log_7 49 = 2 \)
C \( \log_{49} 7 = 2 \)
D \( \log_7 2 = 49 \)
The base stays the same (7), the exponent (2) becomes the answer, and the result (49) is the argument of the log.

Question 2: Express \( 10^3 = 1000 \) in logarithmic form.

A \( \log_3 1000 = 10 \)
B \( \log 1000 = 10 \)
C \( \log_{10} 1000 = 3 \)
D \( \log_{1000} 10 = 3 \)
\( 10^3 = 1000 \) means the log base 10 of 1000 equals 3. Note: \( \log_{10} \) is often written simply as \( \log \).

Question 3: What is \( \log_5 125 \) equal to?

A 3
B 25
C 5
D \( \frac{1}{3} \)
Ask yourself: "5 to what power gives 125?" Since \( 5^3 = 125 \), therefore \( \log_5 125 = 3 \).

Question 4: Express \( \log_2 16 = 4 \) in exponential form.

A \( 4^2 = 16 \)
B \( 16^2 = 4 \)
C \( 2^{16} = 4 \)
D \( 2^4 = 16 \)
The base of the log (2) becomes the base of the exponential. The answer (4) becomes the exponent, and the argument (16) is the result.

Question 5: What is the value of \( \log_a 1 \) for any valid base \( a \)?

A 1
B 0
C \( a \)
D Undefined
\( \log_a 1 = 0 \) because \( a^0 = 1 \) for any valid base \( a \). This is a fundamental property of logarithms.

Question 6: What is the value of \( \log_a a \) for any valid base \( a \)?

A 1
B 0
C \( a \)
D \( a^2 \)
\( \log_a a = 1 \) because \( a^1 = a \). Any base raised to the power 1 equals itself.

Question 7: What is the inverse of \( f(x) = 3^x \)?

A \( f^{-1}(x) = x^3 \)
B \( f^{-1}(x) = \log_x 3 \)
C \( f^{-1}(x) = \log_3 x \)
D \( f^{-1}(x) = 3^{-x} \)
The inverse of an exponential function \( a^x \) is the logarithmic function \( \log_a x \) with the same base.

Question 8: What is the domain of \( y = \log_2 x \)?

A \( x \in \mathbb{R} \) (all real numbers)
B \( x \geq 0 \)
C \( x \geq 1 \)
D \( x > 0 \)
The domain of any logarithmic function is \( x > 0 \). You cannot take the log of zero or a negative number.

Question 9: What is the x-intercept of \( y = \log_5 x \)?

A (0, 0)
B (1, 0)
C (5, 0)
D There is no x-intercept
The x-intercept is where \( y = 0 \). Since \( \log_a 1 = 0 \) for any valid base, the x-intercept is always at (1, 0).

Question 10: If \( \log_2 x = -3 \), what is the value of \( x \)?

A \( -8 \)
B \( -6 \)
C \( \frac{1}{8} \)
D \( 8 \)
Convert to exponential form: \( 2^{-3} = x \). Therefore \( x = \frac{1}{2^3} = \frac{1}{8} \).
