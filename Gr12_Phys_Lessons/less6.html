Lesson 6

Elastic and Inelastic Collisions

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Understand and identify the two types of collisions: elastic and inelastic collisions
  • Distinguish between elastic and inelastic collisions using kinetic energy calculations
  • Apply the law of conservation of momentum to collision problems
  • Calculate kinetic energy before and after collisions to determine collision type

📖 Overview

In this lesson, we explore what happens to kinetic energy during collisions. While momentum is always conserved in an isolated system, kinetic energy may or may not be conserved depending on the type of collision. Understanding the difference between elastic and inelastic collisions is crucial for analyzing real-world scenarios from car crashes to particle physics.

Key Formula for Kinetic Energy:

Ek = ½mv²

where m = mass (kg), v = velocity (m·s⁻¹), Ek = kinetic energy (J)

Pre-knowledge Required

  • Kinetic energy formula and calculations
  • Conservation of momentum
  • Units of energy (Joules)

💡 Main Concepts & Principles

🟢 Elastic Collision

Definition: A collision in which kinetic energy IS conserved. The total kinetic energy before the collision equals the total kinetic energy after the collision.

Characteristics:

  • Ek(before) = Ek(after)
  • Objects bounce off each other
  • Rare in everyday occurrences (ideal conditions)
  • Examples: Collisions between gas molecules, billiard balls (approximately)

🟠 Inelastic Collision

Definition: A collision in which kinetic energy is NOT conserved. The total kinetic energy before the collision does NOT equal the total kinetic energy after the collision.

Characteristics:

  • Ek(before) ≠ Ek(after)
  • Some kinetic energy is transformed into heat, sound, or deformation
  • Most real-world collisions are inelastic
  • Examples: Car crashes, catching a ball, objects sticking together

⚠️ Important Points to Remember

  • Momentum is ALWAYS conserved in collisions within an isolated system
  • Kinetic energy is usually NOT conserved in real-world collisions
  • In inelastic collisions, the "lost" kinetic energy is transformed into heat and sound
  • To determine collision type: Calculate Ek before and after, then compare

📝 Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Vehicle Collision

Problem: At rush hour in Johannesburg, vehicle A (2000 kg) stopped as the robot turned red. Vehicle B (2400 kg), speeding behind A, was unable to stop and crashed into the back of A at 12 m·s⁻¹. Vehicle A was pushed into the intersection and B slowed down to 5 m·s⁻¹.

a) Assume that momentum was conserved and calculate the speed of A immediately after the collision.

b) Determine by calculation whether this was an elastic or inelastic collision.

Step 1: Identify Given Information

m₁ = 2000 kg (Vehicle A), v₁ᵢ = 0 m·s⁻¹ (stationary)

m₂ = 2400 kg (Vehicle B), v₂ᵢ = 12 m·s⁻¹, v₂f = 5 m·s⁻¹

Step 2: Apply Conservation of Momentum (Part a)

pbefore = pafter

m₁v₁ᵢ + m₂v₂ᵢ = m₁v₁f + m₂v₂f

(2000 × 0) + (2400 × 12) = (2000 × v₁f) + (2400 × 5)

0 + 28800 = 2000v₁f + 12000

2000v₁f = 16800

v₁f = 8.4 m·s⁻¹

Step 3: Calculate Kinetic Energy Before (Part b)

Ek(before) = ½m₁v₁ᵢ² + ½m₂v₂ᵢ²

= ½(2000)(0)² + ½(2400)(12)²

= 0 + 172800

Ek(before) = 1.73 × 10⁵ J

Step 4: Calculate Kinetic Energy After

Ek(after) = ½m₁v₁f² + ½m₂v₂f²

= ½(2000)(8.4)² + ½(2400)(5)²

= 70560 + 30000

Ek(after) = 1.01 × 10⁵ J

Step 5: Conclusion

Since Ek(before) (1.73 × 10⁵ J) ≠ Ek(after) (1.01 × 10⁵ J)

The collision is INELASTIC

Energy lost = 1.73 × 10⁵ - 1.01 × 10⁵ = 7.2 × 10⁴ J (converted to heat, sound, deformation)

Worked Example 2: Toy Car Collision

Problem: A toy car of mass 0.5 kg travelling at 2 m·s⁻¹ collides with a stationary lorry of mass 0.8 kg. The lorry is pushed ahead at 0.5 m·s⁻¹. What is the final velocity of the car? Prove that the collision is inelastic.

Step 1: Identify Given Information

m₁ = 0.5 kg (toy car), v₁ᵢ = 2 m·s⁻¹

m₂ = 0.8 kg (lorry), v₂ᵢ = 0 m·s⁻¹, v₂f = 0.5 m·s⁻¹

Step 2: Apply Conservation of Momentum

pbefore = pafter

m₁v₁ᵢ + m₂v₂ᵢ = m₁v₁f + m₂v₂f

(0.5 × 2) + (0.8 × 0) = (0.5 × v₁f) + (0.8 × 0.5)

1 + 0 = 0.5v₁f + 0.4

0.5v₁f = 0.6

v₁f = 1.2 m·s⁻¹ (in direction of car)

Step 3: Calculate Kinetic Energy Before

Ek(before) = ½m₁v₁ᵢ² + ½m₂v₂ᵢ²

= ½(0.5)(2)² + ½(0.8)(0)²

Ek(before) = 1 J

Step 4: Calculate Kinetic Energy After

Ek(after) = ½m₁v₁f² + ½m₂v₂f²

= ½(0.5)(1.2)² + ½(0.8)(0.5)²

= 0.36 + 0.1

Ek(after) = 0.46 J

Step 5: Conclusion

Since Ek(before) (1 J) ≠ Ek(after) (0.46 J)

The collision is INELASTIC

Worked Example 3: Bullet and Block (Perfectly Inelastic)

Problem: A bullet of mass 20 g is fired horizontally into a stationary wooden block of mass 1 kg. The bullet strikes the wooden block at a velocity of 490 m·s⁻¹. The impact causes the block-bullet system to slide before coming to rest.

a) Name and state the law used to calculate the velocity of the bullet-block system after collision.

b) Calculate the velocity of the block-bullet system immediately after collision.

c) Is this collision elastic or inelastic?

Part a) Law of Conservation of Momentum

Statement: The total linear momentum of an isolated system is constant (conserved) in both magnitude and direction.

Part b) Calculate Final Velocity

m₁ = 0.02 kg (bullet), v₁ᵢ = 490 m·s⁻¹

m₂ = 1 kg (block), v₂ᵢ = 0 m·s⁻¹

Note: Objects stick together (perfectly inelastic)

pbefore = pafter

m₁v₁ᵢ + m₂v₂ᵢ = (m₁ + m₂)vf

(0.02 × 490) + (1 × 0) = (0.02 + 1)vf

9.8 = 1.02vf

vf = 9.61 m·s⁻¹

Part c) Determine Collision Type

Ek(before) = ½(0.02)(490)² + ½(1)(0)² = 2401 J

Ek(after) = ½(1.02)(9.61)² = 47.1 J

Since Ek(before) (2401 J) ≠ Ek(after) (47.1 J)

The collision is INELASTIC (specifically, perfectly inelastic since objects stick together)

⚠️ Notice how much energy was lost! (2401 - 47.1 = 2353.9 J converted to heat and deformation)
