Grade 12 Physical Sciences Week 4 Lesson 3 Safety Applications

TOPIC: Momentum and Impulse

Lesson 3: Impulse and Safety Applications

Apply the principles of impulse to understand safety features in vehicles and solve integrated problems combining projectile motion, momentum, and impulse.

🎯 Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Apply impulse concepts to safety considerations in everyday life
  • Explain how airbags, seatbelts, crumple zones, and arrestor beds work using physics
  • Solve integrated problems combining impulse, momentum, and projectile motion
  • Apply the conservation of momentum in collision problems
  • Calculate forces during collisions using impulse-momentum relationships

📖 Overview

In this lesson, we bring together multiple physics concepts to solve real-world problems. Exam questions on momentum and impulse are typically not asked in isolation – they combine with projectile motion and conservation of momentum to create challenging, integrated problems.

📌 Integrated Problem-Solving

Questions in your exams will typically combine:

  • Vertical projectile motion (to find velocities)
  • Impulse calculations (FnetΔt = Δp)
  • Conservation of momentum (in collisions)
  • Kinetic energy calculations
Pre-knowledge Required:
  • Impulse: FnetΔt = Δp
  • Conservation of momentum: Total momentum before = Total momentum after (in isolated systems)
  • Projectile motion equations: vf² = vi² + 2aΔy
  • Kinetic energy: Ek = ½mv²

Baseline Questions

Think about these before continuing:
  1. In the equation Fnet = Δp/Δt, what is the relationship between Fnet and Δt?
  2. State the principle of conservation of momentum.
Answers:
  • Fnet is inversely proportional to Δt (when Δp is constant)
  • The total linear momentum in an isolated system is constant in both magnitude and direction.

🛡️ Impulse and Motor Vehicle Safety

During a collision where a car is brought to rest, the people in the car must also be brought to rest. The key insight from the impulse equation is:

Fnet = Δp / Δt
If Δt increases → Fnet decreases (for the same Δp)
The Safety Principle: By increasing the time over which a collision occurs, we can significantly reduce the force experienced by the occupants, thus reducing injury.

Safety Features Explained

🚗

Crumple Zones

The front and rear of a car are designed to crumple during a collision. While the front crumples, the strong central cab (with passengers) continues moving for a little longer while slowing down.

Physics: Increases Δt → Decreases F on passengers
💨

Airbags

When deployed, the airbag begins to exert a retarding force on the person. As the air compresses, it extends the collision time significantly.

Physics: Spreads force over longer time, cushions impact
🪢

Seatbelts

Seatbelts restrain passengers and prevent them from colliding with hard surfaces. They also stretch slightly, increasing the stopping time.

Physics: Prevents secondary collision + increases Δt
🛤️

Arrestor Beds

Sand or gravel beds at the end of runaway truck ramps. The loose material increases the stopping distance (and time) for out-of-control vehicles.

Physics: Greatly increases Δt → Much smaller F
Remember: All these safety features work on the same principle – they increase the collision time to reduce the force on occupants. The momentum change (Δp) stays the same!

📚 Worked Examples: Integrated Problems

1 Worked Example: Falling Ball (Projectile Motion + Impulse)
Problem: The roof of a tall building is 25 m above the ground. A rigid ball of mass 0.3 kg falls freely when dropped from the roof. It strikes the concrete floor on the ground with velocity v₁. It bounces to a maximum vertical height of 6 m. The collision time is 0.9 s.
1.1 Calculate the velocity v₁ when the ball first hits the floor.
1.2 Calculate the impulse on the ball as a result of the collision.
1.3 Calculate the magnitude of the net force exerted on the ball.
Take UP as POSITIVE

1.1 Finding v₁ (velocity just before hitting floor):
Using: vf² = vi² + 2aΔy
vf² = 0² + 2(-9.8)(-25) [Δy is negative - downward]
vf² = 490
vf = -22.13 m·s⁻¹ [negative = downward]
v₁ = 22.13 m·s⁻¹ downward

1.2 Finding the impulse:
First, find velocity just after bounce (v₂):
At max height, v = 0, so:
vf² = vi² + 2aΔy
0² = vi² + 2(-9.8)(6)
vi² = 117.6
vi = +10.84 m·s⁻¹ [positive = upward]

Now calculate impulse:
Impulse = Δp = m(vf - vi)
Impulse = 0.3[10.84 - (-22.13)]
Impulse = 0.3 × 32.97
Impulse = 9.89 N·s upward

1.3 Finding the net force:
FnetΔt = Δp
Fnet(0.9) = 9.89
Fnet = 9.89 / 0.9
Fnet = 10.99 N upward
2 Worked Example: Trolley Collision (Kinetic Energy + Conservation of Momentum + Impulse)
Problem: Two shopping trolleys, X and Y, are both moving to the right along the same straight line. The mass of trolley Y is 12 kg and its kinetic energy is 37.5 J. Trolley X of mass 30 kg collides with trolley Y and they stick together on impact. After the collision, the combined speed of the trolleys is 3.2 m·s⁻¹. (Ignore friction)
2.1 Calculate the speed of trolley Y before the collision.
2.2 Calculate the speed of trolley X before the collision.
2.3 If the collision time is 0.2 s, calculate the magnitude of the force that trolley X exerts on trolley Y.
Take to the RIGHT as POSITIVE

2.1 Finding speed of trolley Y:
Ek = ½mv²
37.5 = ½(12)v²
37.5 = 6v²
v² = 6.25
vY = 2.5 m·s⁻¹

2.2 Finding speed of trolley X (using conservation of momentum):
pbefore = pafter
m₁v₁ᵢ + m₂v₂ᵢ = (m₁ + m₂)vf
30(vX) + (12)(2.5) = (30 + 12)(3.2)
30vX + 30 = 134.4
30vX = 104.4
vX = 3.48 m·s⁻¹

2.3 Finding force on trolley Y:
For trolley Y:
FnetΔt = mY(vf - vYi)
Fnet(0.2) = 12(3.2 - 2.5)
Fnet(0.2) = 12(0.7)
Fnet(0.2) = 8.4
Fnet = 8.4 / 0.2
Fnet = 42 N (to the right)
Note: We can also verify using trolley X:
FnetΔt = mX(vf - vXi)
Fnet(0.2) = 30(3.2 - 3.48) = 30(-0.28) = -8.4
Fnet = -42 N (negative means opposite direction)
This confirms Newton's Third Law: The forces are equal and opposite!
