🚀 Vertical Projectiles

Sub-topic: Projectile Motion & Gravitational Acceleration

Lesson 2: Understanding Projectile Motion

🎯 Learning Objectives

At the end of this lesson, learners should understand the meaning of projectile motion and be able to explain that projectiles:

  • Fall freely with gravitational acceleration "g" (≈ 9.8 m·s⁻²)
  • Accelerate downwards with constant acceleration whether moving upward or downward
  • Have zero velocity at their greatest height (maximum height)
  • Take the same time to reach their greatest height from the point of upward launch as the time they take to fall back to the point of launch
  • Can have their motion described by a single set of kinematic equations

📋 Overview

Vertical projectile motion is a fundamental concept in physics that describes the motion of objects moving under the influence of gravity alone. This lesson explores how objects behave when thrown upward, dropped, or projected vertically, and introduces the mathematical tools needed to analyze such motion.

Key Insight: Whether an object is moving up or down, the acceleration due to gravity always acts downward at approximately 9.8 m·s⁻² on Earth's surface.

📚 Main Concepts & Principles

1. What is Projectile Motion?

A projectile is any object that is given an initial velocity and then follows a path determined entirely by the effects of gravitational acceleration and air resistance (which we typically ignore in calculations).

For vertical projectile motion, the object moves only along the vertical axis (up and down).

2. Key Characteristics of Vertical Projectiles

Gravitational Acceleration (g)

All objects near Earth's surface experience a constant downward acceleration of approximately 9.8 m·s⁻². This acceleration:

  • Is independent of the object's mass
  • Acts downward regardless of the object's direction of motion
  • Remains constant throughout the motion (ignoring air resistance)

Velocity at Maximum Height

When a projectile reaches its maximum height:

  • The velocity is momentarily zero (0 m·s⁻¹)
  • The acceleration is still -9.8 m·s⁻² (unchanged!)
  • The object is about to reverse direction

Symmetry of Projectile Motion

For a projectile that returns to its starting height:

  • Time to rise = Time to fall
  • Initial upward velocity magnitude = Final downward velocity magnitude
  • The motion is symmetric about the maximum height point

3. Equations of Motion

The following kinematic equations describe vertical projectile motion:

vf = vi + gt

Final velocity = Initial velocity + (acceleration × time)

Δy = vit + ½gt²

Displacement = (Initial velocity × time) + ½(acceleration × time²)

vf² = vi² + 2gΔy

Final velocity² = Initial velocity² + 2(acceleration × displacement)

Sign Convention: If you choose UP as positive, then g = -9.8 m·s⁻². If you choose DOWN as positive, then g = +9.8 m·s⁻². Be consistent throughout your calculations!

✏️ Worked Examples

Example 1: Ball thrown upward from a roof

Problem:

A boy stands on a roof and kicks a ball from a position 5 m above the ground. The ball goes vertically into the air with an initial velocity of 4 m·s⁻¹. The ball lands on the ground after 1.8 s.

Taking up as positive:

a) What is the velocity at the highest point?

Answer: v = 0 m·s⁻¹ (velocity is always zero at maximum height)

b) What is the acceleration at this height?

Answer: g = -9.8 m·s⁻² (acceleration due to gravity is constant)

c) How long to reach maximum height?

Using vf = vi + gt

0 = 4 + (-9.8)t

t = 0.41 s

d) Velocity just before striking the ground?

Using vf = vi + gt

vf = 4 + (-9.8)(1.8)

vf = -13.64 m·s⁻¹

vf = 13.64 m·s⁻¹ downward

Example 2: Bouncing ball

Problem:

A ball is dropped from a height of 2.5 m and after bouncing it reaches a height of 1.5 m. Taking up as positive.

a) Initial velocity as dropped:

vi = 0 m·s⁻¹ (dropped, not thrown)

b) Final velocity before striking ground:

Using vf² = vi² + 2gΔy

vf² = 0² + 2(-9.8)(-2.5)

vf² = 49

vf = -7 m·s⁻¹ (negative = downward)

c) Final velocity at 1.5 m after bounce:

vf = 0 m·s⁻¹ (at maximum height)

d) Initial velocity after bounce:

Using vf² = vi² + 2gΔy

0² = vi² + 2(-9.8)(1.5)

vi = 5.42 m·s⁻¹ (upward)

Example 3: Ball released from hot air balloon

Problem:

A hot air balloon rises vertically at constant velocity. When it is 80 m up in the air, a ball is released (not thrown). The ball rises briefly then drops to Earth.

Why does the ball rise initially?

When released, the ball has the same upward velocity as the balloon. It continues to rise but slows down due to gravity. After about 1 second, it stops rising and starts to fall.

If ball reaches 85 m (rises 5 m), time in air before dropping:

Taking downward as positive from point B to C:

vi = 0 m·s⁻¹, y = -5 m, g = -9.8 m·s⁻²

Using y = vit + ½gt²

-5 = 0 + ½(-9.8)t²

t = 1.01 s

Initial velocity of ball (velocity of balloon):

Using vf = vi + gt

0 = vi + (-9.8)(1.01)

vi = 9.9 m·s⁻¹

💡 Important Points & Exam Tips

If an object is dropped, its initial velocity is zero. If it is thrown or projected, its initial velocity will not be zero.
When a projectile reaches maximum height above the ground, its velocity is momentarily zero until it starts dropping down.
If you have a choice, it is usually easiest to take up as positive. This means g = -9.8 m·s⁻² and upward velocities are positive.
Units must always be written next to the final answer. Do not write units within your calculation - only at the end!
The acceleration of a projectile is always -9.8 m·s⁻² (taking up as positive), even at maximum height when velocity is zero!
