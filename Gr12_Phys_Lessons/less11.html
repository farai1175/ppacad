Overview

Power is a fundamental concept in physics that measures how quickly work is performed or energy is transferred. Understanding power is essential for analyzing machines, engines, and electrical devices in everyday life.

While two machines might do the same amount of work, the one that does it faster has greater power. This concept has practical applications in everything from choosing a car engine to designing efficient electrical systems.

💡

Main Concepts & Principles

1. Definition of Power

Power is defined as the rate at which work is done or energy is transferred.

Formula 1: Power from Work
P = W / Δt = ΔE / Δt

Where: P = Power (W), W = Work done (J), ΔE = Energy transferred (J), Δt = Time (s)

⚡ SI Unit: Watt (W)

1 Watt = 1 Joule per second (J·s⁻¹)

One watt is the rate of working or energy transfer of one joule per second.

📐 Scalar Quantity

Power has magnitude only - no direction.

Unlike force or velocity, power doesn't have a direction associated with it.

🔋 Common Units

1 kW = 1000 W

1 MW = 1,000,000 W

1 hp ≈ 746 W

2. Power and Constant Velocity

When an object moves at constant velocity, we can derive an alternative formula for power:

Derivation:

Since W = F·Δx, and we divide both sides by Δt:

P = W/Δt = F·Δx/Δt = F·v

Formula 2: Power from Force and Velocity
Pav = F · vav

Where: Pav = Average power (W), F = Force (N), vav = Average velocity (m·s⁻¹)

⚠️ Important: This formula applies when force and velocity act along the same straight line, and the velocity is either constant or an average value.

3. Efficiency

Efficiency tells us how much of the input energy actually did useful work. The rest is typically dissipated as heat.

Formula 3: Efficiency
Efficiency = (Useful Work Output / Total Work Input) × 100%

✅ Key Points

  • Efficiency is expressed as a percentage
  • It's always less than 100% due to energy losses
  • Energy is often "lost" as heat, sound, or friction

🔧 Real-World Examples

  • Electric motors: 80-95%
  • Car engines: 20-35%
  • LED lights: ~40-50%

Worked Examples

📝 Example 1: Car Acceleration
Problem: A car with a mass of 1 200 kg accelerates from rest at 5 m·s⁻² across a horizontal road. Calculate the average power of the engine if the car accelerates for 5 s.
Solution:
Step 1: Calculate final velocity using motion equation
vf = vi + aΔt = 0 + (5)(5) = 25 m·s⁻¹
Step 2: Calculate average velocity
vav = (vi + vf)/2 = (0 + 25)/2 = 12.5 m·s⁻¹
Step 3: Calculate power using P = F·v = ma·v
P = (1 200)(5)(12.5) = 75 000 W = 75 kW
📝 Example 2: Constant Speed Motion
Problem: A car moves at a constant speed of 20 m·s⁻¹. The frictional force equals the driving force. Determine the frictional force F if it has an 88 kW motor.
Solution:
Step 1: Use P = F·v
88 000 = F × 20
Step 2: Solve for F
F = 88 000 / 20 = 4 400 N
📝 Example 3: Efficiency Calculation
Problem: A 100 W electric motor takes 1 minute to lift a 50 kg mass vertically through 6 m at constant speed. Calculate the efficiency of the motor.
Solution:
Step 1: Calculate work input (electrical energy)
Winput = P × Δt = 100 × 60 = 6 000 J
Step 2: Calculate work output (useful work = gravitational PE)
Woutput = mgh = (50)(9.8)(6) = 2 940 J
Step 3: Calculate efficiency
Efficiency = (2 940 / 6 000) × 100 = 49%
📝 Example 4: Mine Elevator
Problem: An elevator lowers 14 miners (average mass 70 kg each) plus the lift (mass 1 500 kg) down a mine shaft of 300 m in 100 seconds. Calculate the power the elevator engine must generate.
Solution:
Step 1: Calculate total mass
mminers = 14 × 70 = 980 kg
mtotal = 980 + 1 500 = 2 480 kg
Step 2: Calculate work done against gravity
W = mgh = (2 480)(9.8)(300) = 7 291 200 J
Step 3: Calculate power
P = W/Δt = 7 291 200 / 100 = 72 912 W ≈ 73 kW
