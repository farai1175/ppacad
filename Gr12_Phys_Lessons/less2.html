TOPIC: Momentum and Impulse

Lesson 2: The Meaning of Impulse

Discover the relationship between force, time, and change in momentum through the concept of impulse.

🎯 Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Understand and explain the meaning of impulse
  • Define impulse using the equation: Impulse = FnetΔt = Δp
  • Apply Newton's Second Law in terms of momentum
  • Perform calculations involving momentum, impulse, and force
  • Understand the practical applications of impulse in real-world scenarios

📖 Overview

In this lesson, we explore impulse – a concept that connects force, time, and the change in momentum of an object. Isaac Newton realized that the relationship between these quantities is fundamental to understanding motion and collisions.

Pre-knowledge Required: Before studying impulse, ensure you understand:
  • Newton's Second Law of Motion: Fnet = ma
  • Momentum: p = mv
  • Change in momentum: Δp = m(vf - vi)

Baseline Questions

Before we begin, consider these questions:

  • What is Newton's Second Law equation?
  • What is the equation for determining change in momentum?

📚 Main Concepts & Principles

Newton's Insight on Force and Momentum

Isaac Newton realized a crucial relationship:

Newton's Key Insight: The larger the change of momentum and the shorter the time it takes place in, the larger the force necessary to bring it about.

Newton's Second Law of Motion (in terms of momentum)

The rate of change of momentum of a body is directly proportional to the net force acting on it, and the change of momentum is in the direction of the force.

Deriving the Impulse Equation

From Newton's Second Law, we can express force in terms of momentum:

Fnet = Δp / Δt
Net Force = Change in Momentum ÷ Change in Time

Rearranging this equation gives us:

Fnet × Δt = Δp
Impulse = Change in Momentum

What is Impulse?

The product Fnet × Δt is called the impulse delivered by the force.

Impulse = Fnet × Δt
Unit: Newton-second (N·s) | Also equivalent to kg·m·s⁻¹
Key Properties of Impulse:
  • Impulse is a vector quantity (has magnitude and direction)
  • Unit: N·s (Newton-seconds) or equivalently kg·m·s⁻¹
  • Impulse equals the change in momentum: Impulse = Δp
  • Direction of impulse is the same as the direction of the net force

Comparing Momentum and Impulse

Property Momentum (p) Impulse (J)
Formula p = mv J = FnetΔt = Δp
Unit kg·m·s⁻¹ N·s or kg·m·s⁻¹
Type Vector Vector
Describes Quantity of motion Effect of force over time

Practical Significance

Why Impulse Matters: The impulse equation shows that the same change in momentum can be achieved by:
  • A large force acting for a short time, OR
  • A small force acting for a long time

This is why airbags and crumple zones in cars save lives – they increase the collision time, reducing the force on passengers!

1 Worked Example: Calculating Impulse and Change in Momentum
Problem: The driver of a car of mass 1200 kg travelling at 20 m·s⁻¹ along a straight road slows down uniformly for 4 s. The brakes apply a net force of 4800 N.
  1. Calculate the impulse exerted by the brakes.
  2. What is the change in momentum of the car?
  3. Calculate the velocity, vf, of the car after 4 s.
Solution:

a) Impulse:
Impulse = Fnet × Δt
Impulse = -4800 × 4 (negative because braking opposes motion)
Impulse = -19 200 N·s

b) Change in momentum:
Change in momentum = Impulse
Δp = -19 200 kg·m·s⁻¹

c) Final velocity:
FnetΔt = m(vf - vi)
-19 200 = 1200(vf - 20)
-19 200 = 1200vf - 24 000
1200vf = -19 200 + 24 000
1200vf = 4 800
vf = 4 m·s⁻¹
2 Worked Example: Ball Colliding with Wall
Problem: A rubber ball with a mass of 80 g is thrown horizontally and collides perpendicularly with a wall at a velocity of 7 m·s⁻¹. The ball rebounds in the opposite direction at 6 m·s⁻¹.
  1. Calculate the magnitude and state the direction of the change in momentum of the ball during the collision.
  2. If the collision lasts for 0.02 s, calculate the magnitude of the force the ball experiences during the collision.
Solution:
Take towards the wall as positive
Given: m = 80 g = 0.08 kg, vi = +7 m·s⁻¹, vf = -6 m·s⁻¹, Δt = 0.02 s

a) Change in momentum:
Δp = m(vf - vi)
Δp = 0.08(-6 - 7)
Δp = 0.08 × (-13)
Δp = -1.04 kg·m·s⁻¹
Δp = 1.04 kg·m·s⁻¹ away from the wall

b) Force during collision:
FnetΔt = Δp
Fnet(0.02) = -1.04
Fnet = -1.04 / 0.02
Fnet = -52 N
Fnet = 52 N away from the wall
3 Worked Example: Car Crash with and without Airbags
Problem: A student of mass 75 kg driving a car of mass 1000 kg at 72 km·h⁻¹ skidded on a wet road and hit a tree. The collision took 0.5 s.
  1. Determine the force exerted by the tree on the car.
  2. The car was fitted with airbags, so for the driver, the collision time increased to 1.5 s. Determine the force exerted by the airbags on the driver.
  3. If there had been no airbags, determine the force exerted on the driver by the windscreen (assume collision time = 0.5 s).
Solution:
Take towards the tree as positive
First, convert velocity: vi = 72 km·h⁻¹ = 72 ÷ 3.6 = 20 m·s⁻¹
vf = 0 m·s⁻¹ (car comes to rest)

a) Force on car:
mtotal = 1000 + 75 = 1075 kg
FnetΔt = m(vf - vi)
Fnet(0.5) = 1075(0 - 20)
Fnet(0.5) = -21 500
Fnet = -43 000 N
Fnet = 43 000 N away from the tree

b) Force on driver WITH airbags (Δt = 1.5 s):
mdriver = 75 kg
FnetΔt = m(vf - vi)
Fnet(1.5) = 75(0 - 20)
Fnet(1.5) = -1500
Fnet = -1000 N
Fnet = 1 000 N away from the tree

c) Force on driver WITHOUT airbags (Δt = 0.5 s):
FnetΔt = m(vf - vi)
Fnet(0.5) = 75(0 - 20)
Fnet(0.5) = -1500
Fnet = -3000 N
Fnet = 3 000 N away from the tree

Important Observation: The airbag reduced the force on the driver from 3 000 N to 1 000 N – a 3× reduction – simply by increasing the collision time from 0.5 s to 1.5 s!
