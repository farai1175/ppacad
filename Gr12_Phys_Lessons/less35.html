Emission and Absorption Spectra

Lesson 4: Understanding Atomic Spectra

Lesson Progress: 0%

🎯 Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Define and explain continuous emission spectrum
  • Define and explain line emission spectrum
  • Define and explain line absorption spectrum
  • Explain the source of atomic emission spectra and their unique relationship to each element
  • Relate spectral lines to electron transitions between energy levels
  • Explain the difference between atomic absorption and emission spectra
  • Use E = hf to determine photon energy for spectral lines

📚 Overview

When we analyze light from different sources, we discover that not all light contains the same combination of wavelengths. By passing light through a prism or diffraction grating, we can separate it into its component colors, creating what we call a spectrum. The pattern of this spectrum tells us a great deal about the source of the light and the atoms involved.

🌈
Key Insight: Each element has its own unique spectral "fingerprint" - a specific pattern of lines that can be used to identify that element. This is the basis of spectroscopy, a powerful tool in chemistry and astronomy!

🔄 Pre-Knowledge Review

Before studying spectra, let's review some essential concepts:

Essential Background Knowledge

  • Spectrum of light: The rainbow-like series of colors produced by splitting light into its component wavelengths.
  • Violet light: Has the shortest wavelength and is refracted the most.
  • Red light: Has the longest wavelength and is refracted the least.
  • Photon energy: E = hf (where h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s)
  • Energy levels: Electrons in atoms can only exist at specific energy levels.

📖 The Three Types of Spectra

1. Continuous Emission Spectrum

What it looks like: A complete rainbow of colors with no gaps - all wavelengths present.

How it forms: When a solid, liquid, or dense gas is heated until it glows (like a light bulb filament or the sun).

2. Line Emission Spectrum

What it looks like: Bright colored lines on a dark background at specific wavelengths.

How it forms: When a low-pressure gas is heated or has electricity passed through it (like neon signs).

3. Line Absorption Spectrum

What it looks like: Dark lines on a continuous rainbow background at specific wavelengths.

How it forms: When white light passes through a cool gas before reaching the prism.

🔥 Continuous Emission Spectrum

How Does It Form?

  1. Heating begins: When an object is heated, particles begin to vibrate.
  2. Red hot: Initially, red light (lowest frequency in visible spectrum) is emitted. The object appears "red hot."
  3. Getting hotter: As temperature increases, particles vibrate faster and emit light of higher frequencies.
  4. White hot: When very hot, atoms vibrate at ALL possible frequencies, emitting all colors of visible light.
  5. Continuous spectrum: When this white light passes through a prism, a full, continuous rainbow is observed.
💡
Examples: Incandescent light bulbs, the Sun's core, molten metal, and hot coals all produce continuous emission spectra.

✨ Line Emission Spectrum

How Does It Form?

  • When a single element is heated until it emits light, or when an electric current passes through a gas...
  • The gas emits light of only specific frequencies
  • When passed through a prism, this creates bright colored lines on a dark background
  • Each element produces its own unique pattern of lines - like a fingerprint!

Why Only Specific Frequencies?

Atoms have discrete energy levels. When electrons jump from higher to lower energy levels, they release photons with specific energies:

Ephoton = E2 - E1 = hf

Since energy levels are fixed, only certain photon energies (and thus frequencies) can be emitted!

🔬
Examples: Neon signs (orange-red), sodium street lamps (yellow), hydrogen discharge tubes (purple/violet).

🌑 Line Absorption Spectrum

How Does It Form?

  • White light (containing all frequencies) passes through a cool gas
  • Atoms in the gas absorb specific wavelengths that match their electron transition energies
  • These absorbed wavelengths appear as dark lines on the continuous spectrum
  • The dark lines appear at the same positions as the bright lines in the emission spectrum of that element

The Connection Between Emission and Absorption

The dark lines in an absorption spectrum appear at exactly the same wavelengths as the bright lines in the emission spectrum of the same element. This is because the same electron transitions are involved - just in opposite directions!

🌟
Application: Scientists use absorption spectra to identify elements in distant stars! The dark lines in starlight tell us what elements are present in the star's atmosphere.

⚡ Energy Levels and Electron Transitions

Einstein's Contribution

In 1905, Einstein suggested that electromagnetic radiation consists of particles called photons, each with energy:

Photon Energy

E = hf

E = energy of photon (J)

h = Planck's constant = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s

f = frequency of light (Hz)

Electron Transitions

When atoms emit photons with only specific energies, it tells us that:

  • The internal energy of an atom exists at discrete energy levels
  • Energy can only increase or decrease in specific "jumps"
  • When an electron drops from a higher level (E₂) to a lower level (E₁), it emits a photon

Energy of Emitted Photon

Ephoton = E2 - E1

E₂ = higher energy level (J)

E₁ = lower energy level (J)

The photon carries away the difference in energy

✏️ Worked Examples

Example 1: Calculating Photon Energy from Wavelength

Question: The wavelength of the indigo line in a hydrogen spectrum is roughly 434 nm. Calculate the energy of a photon of indigo light.

1 First, find the frequency using c = fλ:

c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s

λ = 434 nm = 434 × 10⁻⁹ m = 4.34 × 10⁻⁷ m

2 Calculate frequency:

f = c/λ = (3 × 10⁸)/(4.34 × 10⁻⁷)

f = 6.91 × 10¹⁴ Hz

3 Calculate photon energy using E = hf:

E = hf = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴)(6.91 × 10¹⁴)

E = 4.58 × 10⁻¹⁹ J

Answer: The energy of an indigo photon is 4.58 × 10⁻¹⁹ J

Example 2: Energy Level Transition

Question: An electron in a hydrogen atom drops from energy level E₃ = -2.42 × 10⁻¹⁹ J to E₂ = -5.45 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. Calculate:

(a) The energy of the released photon

(b) The frequency of the emitted light

1 Part (a) - Energy of photon:

Ephoton = E₃ - E₂

Ephoton = (-2.42 × 10⁻¹⁹) - (-5.45 × 10⁻¹⁹)

Ephoton = 3.03 × 10⁻¹⁹ J

2 Part (b) - Frequency of light:

E = hf, therefore f = E/h

f = (3.03 × 10⁻¹⁹)/(6.63 × 10⁻³⁴)

f = 4.57 × 10¹⁴ Hz

Answers:
(a) Ephoton = 3.03 × 10⁻¹⁹ J
(b) f = 4.57 × 10¹⁴ Hz (This is in the red/orange region of the visible spectrum)
