Baseline Knowledge Check

Ohm's Law: The current through a conductor between two points is directly proportional to the voltage across the two points, provided the temperature remains constant.

V = IR

What You'll Learn

In this lesson, you'll discover that all real batteries have internal resistance - a concept that explains why batteries don't behave like ideal voltage sources. You'll learn how to calculate the effects of internal resistance on circuit behavior and solve complex circuit problems.

📖 Main Concepts & Principles

▼

🔋 Internal Resistance

All real batteries have internal resistance (r). This is due to the resistance of the chemicals and materials inside the battery. The internal resistance is treated as another resistor in series with the external resistance.

⚡ EMF and Terminal Potential Difference

The electromotive force (emf, ε) is the total energy per unit charge supplied by the battery. The voltage is divided between the internal and external resistances:

ε = Vload + Vinternal

Since V = IR for each component:

ε = IRext + Ir = I(Rext + r)

📊 Key Relationships

  • Terminal potential difference (Vload) = Potential difference across the battery terminals when current flows
  • Vload is always less than the battery's emf when current flows
  • "Lost volts" = Vinternal = Ir (voltage dropped across internal resistance)
  • Vload = ε - Ir

📝 Worked Example

A battery in a circuit has an internal resistance r. When switch S is closed, the power dissipation in R₄ is 13.5 W and voltmeter V₂ reads 18 V. When switch S is opened, voltmeter V₁ reads 45.9 V.

a) Calculate the resistance of R₄

Using P = V²/R:
R₄ = V²/P = (18)²/13.5 = 324/13.5 = 24 Ω
b) Calculate the current through the ammeter

Using I = V/R:
I = 18/24 = 0.75 A
c) Calculate the internal resistance r

When switch is open, V₁ = emf = 45.9 V (no current, no internal voltage drop)
When switch is closed: ε = Vload + Ir
45.9 = 18 + (0.75)r
27.9 = 0.75r
r = 27.9/0.75 = 37.2 Ω
