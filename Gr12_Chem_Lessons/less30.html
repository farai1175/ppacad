Overview

▼

📌 Pre-Knowledge Required

This lesson builds on your understanding of:

  • Galvanic cells and their operation
  • Oxidation and reduction half-reactions
  • Acids and bases (ionisation)

When you connect a voltmeter to a galvanic cell, you can measure a potential difference (voltage) between the two electrodes. This voltage represents the "driving force" that pushes electrons through the circuit.

But where does this potential come from? Each half-cell has its own characteristic potential called the standard electrode potential. In this lesson, we'll learn how to measure and use these values.

🌡️

Standard Conditions

▼

For electrode potentials to be comparable, they must be measured under the same conditions. These are called standard conditions:

⚙️ Standard Conditions for Electrode Potentials

🧪
Concentration: 1 mol·dm⁻³ for all solutions
💨
Pressure: 101.3 kPa (1 atm) for gases
🌡️
Temperature: 298 K (25°C)
📝 Definition: Standard Electrode Potential (E°)

The standard electrode potential is the measure of an electrode's tendency to undergo reduction or oxidation when connected in a galvanic cell, measured under standard conditions relative to the standard hydrogen electrode.

⚡

The Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE)

▼

To measure electrode potentials, we need a reference point. The standard hydrogen electrode (SHE) serves as this reference and is assigned a potential of exactly 0.00 V.

Standard Hydrogen Electrode

H₂ gas at 101.3 kPa
Pt electrode
1 mol·dm⁻³ HCl (H⁺ ions)
0.00 V
E° = 0.00 V

Components of the SHE

🧪

Electrolyte

1 mol·dm⁻³ strong acid solution (e.g., HCl)

The acid ionises completely to release H⁺ (or H₃O⁺) ions

⚙️

Electrode

Platinum (Pt) electrode

Inert metal that doesn't react but allows electron transfer

💨

Gas

Hydrogen gas at 101.3 kPa

Bubbled over the platinum electrode

The Half-Reaction

2H⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ ⇌ H₂(g)      E° = 0.00 V

⚠️ Important: How SHE Works

When the SHE is connected to another half-cell:

  • The SHE can act as either the anode or cathode depending on the other half-cell
  • The voltmeter reading shows the standard electrode potential of the other electrode
  • A positive reading means the other electrode is a weaker reducing agent than H₂
  • A negative reading means the other electrode is a stronger reducing agent than H₂
📝 Exercise 1: Understanding the SHE
  1. Explain what is meant by the phrase "electrode potential of a substance"?
    → It is the measure of an electrode's tendency to undergo reduction or oxidation when connected in a galvanic cell.
  2. What standard conditions must be obeyed when measuring electrode potentials?
    → Concentration: 1 mol·dm⁻³, Pressure: 101.3 kPa, Temperature: 298 K (25°C)
  3. A learner measured the cell potential of zinc connected to the SHE and got -0.76 V. What does this tell us?
    → A negative reading of -0.76 V means Zn is a stronger reducing agent than H₂. Zn will undergo oxidation more readily than H₂.

Negative E° Values

  • Metal is a stronger reducing agent than H₂
  • Metal loses electrons more easily
  • Will be the anode when paired with SHE
  • More reactive metals

Positive E° Values

  • Metal ion is a stronger oxidising agent than H⁺
  • Metal ion gains electrons more easily
  • Will be the cathode when paired with SHE
  • Less reactive metals

Calculating the EMF of a Galvanic Cell

▼

The electromotive force (EMF) or cell potential (E°cell) represents the "driving force" that allows electrons to flow between half-cells.

📝 Formula for Calculating EMF
E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode

Remember:

  • Cathode = where reduction occurs (higher E° value)
  • Anode = where oxidation occurs (lower E° value)

💡 Spontaneity of Reactions

  • If E°cell is positive, the reaction is spontaneous
  • If E°cell is negative, the reaction is non-spontaneous

Worked Examples

Example 1: Iron-Silver Cell

Calculate the cell potential for an iron-silver galvanic cell.

Step 1: Identify the E° values from the table
Fe²⁺/Fe: E° = -0.44 V
Ag⁺/Ag: E° = +0.80 V
Step 2: Identify cathode and anode
Cathode (higher E°): Ag⁺/Ag = +0.80 V
Anode (lower E°): Fe²⁺/Fe = -0.44 V
Step 3: Apply the formula
E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode
E°cell = 0.80 − (−0.44)
E°cell = 0.80 + 0.44
E°cell = 1.24 V ✓
Example 2: Magnesium-Copper Cell

Calculate the cell potential for a magnesium-copper galvanic cell.

Step 1: Identify the E° values
Mg²⁺/Mg: E° = -2.37 V
Cu²⁺/Cu: E° = +0.34 V
Step 2: Identify cathode and anode
Cathode: Cu²⁺/Cu = +0.34 V
Anode: Mg²⁺/Mg = -2.37 V
Step 3: Apply the formula
E°cell = 0.34 − (−2.37)
E°cell = 0.34 + 2.37
E°cell = 2.71 V ✓
Example 3: Aluminium-Mercury Cell

Calculate the cell potential for an aluminium-mercury galvanic cell.

Step 1: Identify the E° values
Al³⁺/Al: E° = -1.66 V
Hg²⁺/Hg: E° = +0.79 V
Step 2: Apply the formula
E°cell = 0.79 − (−1.66)
E°cell = 0.79 + 1.66
E°cell = 2.45 V ✓
📝 Exercise 2: Calculate EMF from Cell Notation

Calculate the EMF for the following galvanic cells:

  1. Ca(s)|Ca²⁺(aq)||Cu²⁺(aq)|Cu(s)
    E°cell = 0.34 − (−2.87) = 0.34 + 2.87 = 3.21 V
  2. Mn(s)|Mn²⁺(aq)||Pb²⁺(aq)|Pb(s)
    E°cell = −0.13 − (−1.18) = −0.13 + 1.18 = 1.05 V
  3. Fe(s)|Fe²⁺(aq)||Pt²⁺(aq)|Pt(s)
    E°cell = 1.20 − (−0.44) = 1.20 + 0.44 = 1.64 V
