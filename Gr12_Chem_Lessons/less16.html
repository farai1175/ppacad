Overview

+

The Doppler Effect is the apparent change in frequency of a wave due to relative motion between the source and observer. This phenomenon is commonly experienced with sound waves but applies to all types of waves.

Key Concepts

Frequency: Number of complete waves passing a point per second (Hz)

Pitch: How high or low a sound is perceived (related to frequency)

Relative Velocity: Speed of one object relative to another

Ultrasound: Sound waves with frequency above human hearing (>20 kHz)

Understanding the Doppler Effect

The Doppler Effect is the apparent change in frequency of a wave due to relative motion between the source and observer. The actual frequency produced by the source doesn't change, but the observed frequency does.

Everyday Examples
  • A police car siren sounds higher-pitched as it approaches and lower-pitched as it moves away
  • A train whistle changes pitch as it passes by
  • Race cars sound different when approaching vs. moving away
  • Ambulance sirens demonstrate the effect clearly

Why Does Pitch Change?

Source Approaching Observer:

  • Sound waves get compressed in the direction of motion
  • Wavelength decreases
  • Frequency increases (higher pitch)
  • Waves reach observer more frequently

Source Moving Away from Observer:

  • Sound waves get stretched behind the source
  • Wavelength increases
  • Frequency decreases (lower pitch)
  • Waves reach observer less frequently

Medical Applications of Doppler Effect

Ultrasound in Medicine

Ultrasound refers to sound waves with frequency above human hearing (greater than 20 kHz). These high-frequency waves have small wavelengths that can be reflected by small objects like blood cells and fetal heartbeats.

How it works:

  1. Ultrasound waves are transmitted into the body
  2. Waves reflect off moving blood cells or fetal heartbeat
  3. Reflected waves undergo Doppler shift
  4. Sensitive instruments detect frequency difference
  5. Computer calculates velocity of blood flow or heartbeat

Applications:

  • Measuring rate of blood flow
  • Monitoring fetal heartbeat in the womb
  • Detecting blocked arteries
  • Assessing heart valve function
Other Applications

Sonar (Sound Navigation and Ranging): Uses sound waves to locate underwater objects by measuring the time delay and frequency shift of reflected waves.

Radar (Radio Detection and Ranging): Uses radio waves to detect the speed and location of objects like aircraft and weather patterns.

Astronomy: Measures the redshift or blueshift of light from stars and galaxies to determine their motion relative to Earth.
