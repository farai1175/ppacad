Overview

▼

This lesson introduces the concept of titration — a powerful analytical technique used to determine the concentration of an unknown solution by reacting it with a solution of known concentration. We will explore how to select the correct indicator, understand the end point of neutralization, and perform calculations to find unknown concentrations.

Main Content

▼

1. What is a Titration?

A titration is a technique where a solution of known concentration (called a standard solution) is used to determine the concentration of an unknown solution. This process involves the gradual neutralization of an acid by a base, or a base by an acid.

📌 Key Terms:

  • ✦ Standard Solution: A solution of accurately known concentration
  • ✦ End Point: The point where enough acid/base has been added to bring about a colour change in the indicator
  • ✦ Indicator: A substance that changes colour at a specific pH, showing when neutralization is complete
  • ✦ Volumetric Analysis: The technique of determining concentration using titration

2. Indicators and Their pH Ranges

An indicator is used to determine the end point of a titration. The end point may not necessarily be at pH 7, so choosing the correct indicator for your specific titration is essential.

Indicator pH Range Colour Change
Methyl Orange 3.1 – 4.4 Red → Yellow
Bromothymol Blue 6.0 – 7.6 Yellow → Green → Blue
Phenolphthalein 8.3 – 10 Colourless → Pink/Red

3. Choosing the Correct Indicator

📌 Indicator Selection Rules:

  • 🔵 Strong acid + Strong base (pH ≈ 7) → Use Bromothymol Blue
  • 🔴 Strong acid + Weak base (pH < 7) → Use Methyl Orange
  • 🟣 Weak acid + Strong base (pH > 7) → Use Phenolphthalein
  • 🟢 Weak acid + Weak base (pH ≈ 7) → Use Bromothymol Blue

4. Titration Calculations

When one solution of known concentration is added to a solution of unknown concentration until exact neutralization, we can calculate the unknown concentration using the mole ratio from the balanced equation.

Titration Formula

na/nb = (Ca × Va) / (Cb × Vb)

Where: n = mole ratio, C = concentration (mol·dm⁻³), V = volume (dm³ or cm³)

📝 Worked Example 1

Question: 82 cm³ of hydrochloric acid of concentration 0.2 mol·dm⁻³ is required to neutralize 60 cm³ of Ba(OH)₂ of unknown concentration. Calculate the concentration of Ba(OH)₂.

Step 1: Write the balanced equation and determine the mole ratio
2HCl + Ba(OH)₂ → BaCl₂ + 2H₂O
Mole ratio (acid : base) = 2 : 1
Step 2: Set up the equation
na/nb = (Ca × Va) / (Cb × Vb)

2/1 = (0.2 × 82) / (Cb × 60)
Step 3: Solve for Cb
2 × Cb × 60 = 0.2 × 82
120 × Cb = 16.4
Cb = 16.4 / 120 = 0.137 mol·dm⁻³
📝 Worked Example 2

Question: 32.5 cm³ of a sulphuric acid solution neutralizes exactly 25 cm³ of a potassium hydroxide solution of concentration 0.6 mol·dm⁻³. Calculate the concentration of the acid solution.

Step 1: Write the balanced equation
H₂SO₄ + 2KOH → K₂SO₄ + 2H₂O
Mole ratio (acid : base) = 1 : 2
Step 2: Apply the formula
1/2 = (Ca × 32.5) / (0.6 × 25)
1/2 = (Ca × 32.5) / 15
Step 3: Solve for Ca
Ca × 32.5 = 15 × 0.5 = 7.5
Ca = 7.5 / 32.5 = 0.231 mol·dm⁻³
📝 Worked Example 3

Question: 12.4 g of NaOH is dissolved in 300 cm³ of water. 57 cm³ of this solution neutralizes 30 cm³ of CH₃COOH. Calculate the concentration of the acetic acid.

Step 1: Calculate the concentration of NaOH (M = 40 g/mol)
n = m/M = 12.4/40 = 0.31 mol
C = n/V = 0.31/0.3 = 1.033 mol·dm⁻³
Step 2: Write the balanced equation
NaOH + CH₃COOH → CH₃COONa + H₂O
Mole ratio (base : acid) = 1 : 1
Step 3: Solve for Cacid
1/1 = (1.033 × 57) / (Cacid × 30)
Cacid × 30 = 58.88
Cacid = 58.88 / 30 = 1.96 mol·dm⁻³
