Pre-Knowledge: Intermolecular Forces

Before we explore physical properties, let's recall the types of intermolecular forces:

🔗 London/Dispersion Forces

Weak forces found between all molecules. Caused by temporary dipoles from electron movement. Strength increases with molecular size.

⚡ Dipole-Dipole Forces

Found between polar molecules. The positive end of one molecule attracts the negative end of another.

💧 Hydrogen Bonds

Strong intermolecular forces between H bonded to N, O, or F and the lone pair on another N, O, or F atom. Found in water, alcohols, and carboxylic acids.

🌐 Van der Waals Forces

A general term for weak intermolecular forces including London forces and dipole-dipole forces (but not hydrogen bonds).

Isomerism

📖 Definition: Isomers

Isomers are molecules that have the same molecular formula but different structural formulae (different arrangements of atoms).

📖 Definition: Structural Isomers

Structural isomers are compounds with the same molecular formula but different structural formulae. They may belong to the same homologous series (with similar properties) or different homologous series (with different properties).

Physical Properties of Organic Compounds

The spatial structure of organic molecules determines their physical properties. These properties include:

🌡️
Boiling Point
Temperature at which vapour pressure equals atmospheric pressure
❄️
Melting Point
Temperature at which solid becomes liquid
💨
Vapour Pressure
Tendency of molecules to escape from liquid phase
🍯
Viscosity
Resistance of liquid to flow

📖 Definition: Boiling Point

The boiling point is the temperature at which the vapour pressure of a liquid equals the external (atmospheric) pressure.

📖 Definition: Vapour Pressure

Vapour pressure indicates how easily a liquid evaporates. A substance with high vapour pressure is called volatile. Weaker intermolecular forces = higher vapour pressure = lower boiling point.

📖 Definition: Viscosity

Viscosity is a measure of a liquid's resistance to flow. Stronger intermolecular forces = higher viscosity. Viscosity decreases as temperature increases (hot liquids flow more easily).

Worked Examples

✅ Example 1: Comparing Vapour Pressure

Question: Which has higher vapour pressure: propane (-42°C) or pentane (36°C)?

1 Identify the relationship: Lower boiling point = weaker forces = higher vapour pressure
2 Compare: Propane has a lower boiling point (-42°C < 36°C)
Answer: Propane has higher vapour pressure because it has weaker intermolecular forces and a lower boiling point.

✅ Example 2: Explaining Boiling Point Difference

Question: Explain why pentane (BP: 36°C) has a higher boiling point than 2-methylbutane (BP: 27.8°C).

1 Identify: Both have the same molecular formula (C₅H₁₂) - they are structural isomers
2 Compare structures: Pentane is a straight chain; 2-methylbutane is branched
3 Apply concept: 2-methylbutane is more spherical with fewer points of contact
Answer: 2-methylbutane is more branched, making it more spherical with less surface area for intermolecular forces. Therefore, it has weaker Van der Waals forces and requires less energy to overcome them, resulting in a lower boiling point.

✅ Example 3: Identifying Isomers

Question: Write the IUPAC name for an isomer of propan-1-ol (CH₃CH₂CH₂OH).

1 Identify molecular formula: C₃H₈O
2 Find another arrangement: Move the -OH group to a different carbon
Answer: Propan-2-ol (CH₃CH(OH)CH₃) - the -OH is on the middle carbon instead of the end.
