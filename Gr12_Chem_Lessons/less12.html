📋 Lesson Overview

Chemical reactions occur at different rates. Some reactions, like explosions, happen almost instantaneously, while others, like the rusting of iron, can take years. Understanding what factors influence reaction rates is crucial for controlling chemical processes in industry, medicine, and everyday life.

💡 Key Insight

According to Collision Theory, for a reaction to occur, reactant particles must collide with sufficient energy (activation energy) and the correct orientation. Any factor that increases the frequency or energy of these collisions will increase the reaction rate.

📚 Main Concepts: Factors Affecting Reaction Rates

🌡️ Temperature

Increasing temperature increases the kinetic energy of particles, leading to more frequent and more energetic collisions. This increases the reaction rate.

🧪 Concentration

Higher concentration means more particles per unit volume, increasing the frequency of collisions and thus the reaction rate.

📐 Surface Area

Smaller particles have a larger total surface area exposed to reactants, allowing more collisions to occur simultaneously.

⚗️ Catalyst

A catalyst provides an alternative reaction pathway with lower activation energy, increasing the rate without being consumed.

🔬 Nature of Reactants

Different substances have different bond strengths and molecular structures, affecting how easily they react.

✏️ Worked Examples

📝 Example 1: Antacid Tablet Investigation

Scenario: Antacids neutralize excess stomach acid (a neutralization reaction). When an antacid tablet dissolves in water and reacts with acid, it releases carbon dioxide gas.

Question: Design an investigation to test the effect of temperature on the dissolution rate of an antacid tablet ▼

Investigative Question:

What is the relationship between temperature and the reaction rate of an antacid tablet with water?

Hypothesis:

The reaction rate will increase with an increase in temperature.

Procedure:

  1. Use a measuring cylinder to measure a fixed volume of water (e.g., 100 cm³)
  2. Transfer the water to a beaker
  3. Record the temperature of the water using a thermometer
  4. Add one antacid tablet to the water
  5. Start the stopwatch simultaneously and measure the time for the tablet to completely dissolve
  6. Rinse the beaker and repeat at different temperatures (e.g., 20°C, 40°C, 60°C)
  7. Repeat each temperature trial for accuracy

Conclusion:

It is better to take the antacid with warm water because the rate at which it brings relief will be faster at higher temperatures.

📝 Example 2: Magnesium and Hydrochloric Acid

Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)
Question: Investigating the effect of concentration on reaction rate ▼

Experiment Setup:

  • Experiment 1: 50 cm³ HCl of known concentration with magnesium powder
  • Experiment 2: 25 cm³ HCl diluted to 50 cm³ with distilled water

Analysis:

The concentration in Experiment 2 is SMALLER THAN Experiment 1.

Hypothesis: Reaction rate increases with increase in concentration.

Why use equal amounts of magnesium?

To make a fair comparison/test (controlled variable)

Effect of increasing temperature:

  • Final volume of gas: REMAINS THE SAME (determined by limiting reagent)
  • Volume after 40 seconds: INCREASES (faster rate)

📝 Example 3: Sodium Thiosulphate Investigation

Na₂S₂O₃(aq) + 2HCl(aq) → 2NaCl(aq) + S(s) + H₂O(l) + SO₂(g)
Question: The "disappearing cross" experiment ▼

Method: Add HCl to sodium thiosulphate in a flask placed over a cross on white paper. Measure time for cross to become invisible.

Key Points:

  • Hypothesis: Reaction rate increases with an increase in temperature
  • Hazardous product: Sulphur dioxide (SO₂) - requires well-ventilated room
  • Why cross disappears: Sulphur (S) precipitate forms, making solution cloudy
  • Same observer needed: Different people have different sight abilities and reaction times
