Predicting the Anode and Cathode

▼

The Table of Standard Electrode Potentials is organized in a specific order that helps us predict which electrode will be the anode and which will be the cathode in a galvanic cell.

📝 Key Principle

The Table of Standard Electrode Potentials is arranged from the most negative E° value at the top to the most positive E° value at the bottom.

⬆️

More Negative E° (Top of Table)

  • Stronger reducing agent
  • Metal is more easily oxidised
  • Will be the ANODE
  • Loses electrons more readily
⬇️

More Positive E° (Bottom of Table)

  • Stronger oxidising agent
  • Metal ions are more easily reduced
  • Will be the CATHODE
  • Gains electrons more readily

Reference: Standard Electrode Potentials

Half-Reaction (Reduction) E° (V) Tendency
Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Mg -2.37 Strong reducing agent (easily oxidised) ↑
Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al -1.66
Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Zn -0.76
Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Fe -0.44
Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Pb -0.13
2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂ 0.00 ← Reference
Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu +0.34
Ag⁺ + e⁻ → Ag +0.80 Strong oxidising agent (easily reduced) ↓

💡 Quick Method to Predict Electrodes

  1. Look up the E° values for both metals
  2. The metal with the more negative (or less positive) E° → ANODE (oxidation)
  3. The metal with the more positive (or less negative) E° → CATHODE (reduction)

Worked Examples

Example 1: Magnesium and Iron Cell
Step 1: Look up E° values
Mg²⁺/Mg: E° = -2.37 V
Fe²⁺/Fe: E° = -0.44 V
Step 2: Compare values
Mg has the more negative E° value (-2.37 < -0.44)
Step 3: Predict electrodes
Anode: Mg (oxidation) → Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻
Cathode: Fe (reduction) → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Fe
Example 2: Lead and Iron Cell
Step 1: Look up E° values
Pb²⁺/Pb: E° = -0.13 V
Fe²⁺/Fe: E° = -0.44 V
Step 2: Compare values
Fe has the more negative E° value (-0.44 < -0.13)
Step 3: Predict electrodes
Anode: Fe (oxidation) → Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻
Cathode: Pb (reduction) → Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Pb
Example 3: Iron and Copper Cell
Step 1: Look up E° values
Fe²⁺/Fe: E° = -0.44 V
Cu²⁺/Cu: E° = +0.34 V
Step 2: Compare values
Fe has the more negative E° value (-0.44 < +0.34)
Step 3: Predict electrodes
Anode: Fe (oxidation) → Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻
Cathode: Cu (reduction) → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu

Decomposition of Copper(II) Chloride

▼
🔬 Aim

To investigate the use of an electrolytic cell for the decomposition of copper(II) chloride solution.

Observations

🟤

At the Cathode (−)

The cathode is coated with a small layer of red-brown copper.

💨

At the Anode (+)

Chlorine gas with a pungent odour is released.

Electrode Reactions

Oxidation (Anode): 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻
Reduction (Cathode): Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu

📝 Explanation

At the Cathode: The cations (Cu²⁺) are attracted to the negative cathode where they receive electrons from the power source. By taking up electrons, the Cu²⁺ cation loses its positive charge and becomes copper metal.

At the Anode: The anions (Cl⁻) are attracted to the positive anode where they release electrons. Two chloride ions combine to form chlorine gas.

Guidelines for Predicting Electrolysis Products

🔮 General Guidelines
Metals with a positive E° reduction potential will form at the cathode. Otherwise, hydrogen will be formed from the reduction of water.
Halogens form at the anode when a concentrated solution of a soluble salt of a halide undergoes electrolysis.
A sulfate ion is not oxidised in aqueous solution. Instead, oxygen is formed through oxidation of water or OH⁻ ions.

Electroplating

▼
📝 What is Electroplating?

Electroplating is a process where a thin layer of metal is deposited on another metal, either for protection against oxidation (rusting) or for appearance (aesthetic purposes).

Common Applications

🥄

Silver Plating

Cutlery, jewelry, and decorative items are often plated with silver for appearance.

🔧

Chromium Plating

Car parts, taps, and tools are chrome-plated for corrosion resistance and shine.

💍

Gold Plating

Jewelry and electronics are gold-plated for appearance and conductivity.

How Electroplating Works

🔌 Setup for Silver Electroplating

  • Anode: The pure metal electrode (e.g., pure silver)
  • Cathode: The object to be coated (e.g., brass or nickel spoon)
  • Electrolyte: A solution containing ions of the plating metal (e.g., silver nitrate + potassium cyanide)

Electrode Reactions in Silver Plating

At the Anode: Ag(s) → Ag⁺(aq) + e⁻

Silver dissolves into the solution

At the Cathode: Ag⁺(aq) + e⁻ → Ag(s)

Silver deposits onto the object

💡 Key Point

In electroplating, the same metal is both oxidised and reduced. The anode dissolves while the cathode gets coated. This maintains a constant concentration of metal ions in the electrolyte.

🧪 Role of Cyanide

The cyanide in the electrolyte ensures that there is a low concentration of silver ions, which is essential for effective, even electroplating. A lower ion concentration results in a smoother, more uniform coating.
