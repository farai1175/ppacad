Overview - Baseline Knowledge

▼

🔑 Prerequisite Knowledge Review

Before we begin, let's review equilibrium constants:

Baseline Questions

1. Write down an expression for the equilibrium constant for each reaction:

1.1   2H2O2(aq) ⇌ 2H2O(l) + O2(g)

1.2   2HCl(aq) + CaCO3(s) ⇌ CaCl2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)

Answers:
1.1   Kc = [O2] / [H2O2]²
1.2   Kc = [CO2][CaCl2] / [HCl]²
2. In which reaction will [products] > [reactants] at equilibrium?

2.1   H2(g) + F2(g) ⇌ 2HF(g)     Kc = 1 × 108

2.2   SO2(g) + NO2(g) ⇌ NO(g) + SO3(g)     Kc = 1 × 102

Answers:
2.1   [products] > [reactants] ✓ (Kc >> 1)
2.2   [products] > [reactants] ✓ (Kc > 1)

💡 Key Principle

When Kc > 1, the equilibrium favours products (equilibrium lies to the right).

When Kc < 1, the equilibrium favours reactants (equilibrium lies to the left).

The larger the K value, the more products are formed!

Main Content: Ka and Kb Values

▼

⚖️ Connecting Equilibrium to Acid-Base Strength

The strengths of acids and bases can be compared by using their equilibrium constants (Kc) for the reaction when the acid or base dissolves in water.

🔴 Ka - The Ionisation Constant of Acids

When dealing with the equilibrium constant for pure acids, we use the symbol Ka instead of Kc.

HCl(aq) + H2O(l) ⇌ H3O+(aq) + Cl-(aq)

Ka Expression for this Reaction:

Ka = [H3O+][Cl-] [HCl]

Note: Water is a pure liquid, so it is not included in the expression.

📌 Definition

Ka is called the ionisation constant of the acid. It measures the extent to which an acid ionises in water.

💪 Strong Acids

Ionise completely

→ High [products]

→ HIGH Ka values

Ka >> 1

😐 Weak Acids

Ionise partially

→ Low [products]

→ LOW Ka values

Ka << 1

📊 Example Ka Values

Acid Type Ka Value
HCl (Hydrochloric acid) Strong ~107 (very large)
HNO3 (Nitric acid) Strong ~101 (large)
CH3COOH (Acetic acid) Weak 1.8 × 10-5
H2CO3 (Carbonic acid) Weak 4.3 × 10-7

🔵 Kb - The Dissociation Constant of Bases

When dealing with the equilibrium constant for pure bases, we use the symbol Kb instead of Kc.

NH3(aq) + H2O(l) ⇌ NH4+(aq) + OH-(aq)

Kb Expression for this Reaction:

Kb = [NH4+][OH-] [NH3]

Note: Water is a pure liquid, so it is not included in the expression.

📌 Definition

Kb is called the dissociation constant of the base. It measures the extent to which a base dissociates in water to produce OH- ions.

💪 Strong Bases

Dissociate completely

→ High [products]

→ HIGH Kb values

Kb >> 1

😐 Weak Bases

Dissociate partially

→ Low [products]

→ LOW Kb values

Kb << 1

📊 Example Kb Values

Base Type Kb Value
NaOH (Sodium hydroxide) Strong Very large (>>1)
KOH (Potassium hydroxide) Strong Very large (>>1)
NH3 (Ammonia) Weak 1.8 × 10-5
Ca(OH)2 (Calcium hydroxide) Weak ~10-3

⭐ Key Summary

For Acids:

  • Strong acid → High Ka
  • Weak acid → Low Ka

For Bases:

  • Strong base → High Kb
  • Weak base → Low Kb

Remember: The larger the K value, the stronger the acid or base, because more products (ions) are formed at equilibrium.

Class Exercises

▼

📝 Exercise 1

Determine whether the following acids and bases are strong or weak. Then explain how their ionisation/dissociation constants compare.

a) CH3COOH and HNO3

✅ Answer:

CH3COOH is a weak acid and HNO3 is a strong acid.

HNO3 will have a higher ionisation constant (Ka) than CH3COOH, as it ionises to a greater degree.

Ka(HNO3) >> Ka(CH3COOH)

📝 Exercise 2

b) NaOH and Ca(OH)2

✅ Answer:

NaOH is a strong base and Ca(OH)2 is a weak base.

NaOH will have a higher dissociation constant (Kb) than Ca(OH)2, as it dissociates to a greater degree.

Kb(NaOH) >> Kb(Ca(OH)2)

📝 Exercise 3 - Practice

Rank the following acids from strongest to weakest based on their Ka values:

• Acid A: Ka = 1.5 × 10-3

• Acid B: Ka = 6.8 × 10-10

• Acid C: Ka = 2.1 × 105

• Acid D: Ka = 4.5 × 10-5

✅ Answer:

Strongest to Weakest: C > A > D > B

Explanation:

  • Acid C (Ka = 2.1 × 105) - Strongest (Ka >> 1, strong acid)
  • Acid A (Ka = 1.5 × 10-3) - Moderate weak acid
  • Acid D (Ka = 4.5 × 10-5) - Weaker acid
  • Acid B (Ka = 6.8 × 10-10) - Weakest (very low Ka)
